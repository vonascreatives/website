# How to create a good Topic



## 1\. Clarify Your Purpose and “Why”

**Key question**: Why should someone watch this now?

1. **Identify what’s “new.”** Even if a topic was hot in the news years ago, you have to bring fresh insights or a new angle.

2. **Use a compelling hook.** Viewers want to feel there’s something important, urgent, or undiscovered that justifies watching.

**Example**:

- “How the Philippines Lost One Trillion Pesos in the War on Drugs”

- “Philippines Is a Dystopia? How Duterte, Sara, and the Elite Profit from the Drug War”

These kinds of titles help answer the “why watch it?” question.

---

## 2\. Develop a Strong Hook and Title/Thumbnail

A good title or thumbnail is a viewer’s first contact with your documentary. Here’s how to build one:

1. **Focus on Shocking or Surprising Facts.**

   - “One Trillion Pesos Lost” or “Philippines is a Dystopia” are provocative claims.

2. **Use Familiar Faces and Brands.**

   - Putting recognizable politicians, celebrities, or corporations on the thumbnail or in the title draws immediate interest.

3. **Expose the ‘Untold Secret.’**

   - People love a story that implies you’re revealing something hidden or that the mainstream won’t talk about.

**Practical Tip**:

- In thumbnail design, show a big face (e.g., Duterte) or major company logos (e.g., SM, Ayala, San Miguel). Combine that image with an evocative tagline or question.

## 3\. Outline the Documentary in Segments

Instead of focusing on just three people or three interviews, structure your story into segments. Each segment builds on the previous one. A possible **template**:

1. **Introduction (The Hook)**

   - Grab attention with a surprising statistic or question.

   - Show a short clip or statement from an expert or interviewee that teases the main controversy or problem.

2. **Segment 1: Background & Context**

   - Briefly explain the historical or social context (the War on Drugs, the main actors—Duterte administration, etc.).

   - Add personal or emotional anecdotes but keep them short so the viewer understands the stakes.

3. **Segment 2: The Big Discovery/Investigation**

   - Present the main bold statement or puzzle (e.g., “Philippines lost a trillion pesos… where did it go?”).

   - Introduce data, evidence, or documents that back up the claim.

   - Bring in **experts** (economists, investigative journalists, policy analysts) who can support or counter the claim.

4. **Segment 3: Personal Stories / Case Studies**

   - Invite a former policeman or a convicted drug pusher or a victim’s relative—who each shows different angles of the crisis.

   - Keep it from becoming just a crime story by focusing on broader systemic insights, not merely individual tragedies.

5. **Segment 4: Wider Implications**

   - Explore who benefits from the War on Drugs (e.g., certain powerful families, corruption, possible misallocation of funds).

   - Could also highlight the changes under Sarah Duterte’s leadership or the ongoing legal/political ramifications.

6. **Segment 5: Conclusion / Call to Action**

   - Summarize your findings.

   - Show why it still matters now (“the shadow of the War on Drugs lingers…”).

   - Possibly suggest ways forward or what viewers can do/think about next.

---

## 4\. Use Interviews Strategically

The “talking heads” (guests) should serve the documentary’s main points rather than become the focus themselves. Their roles:

- **Expert Witnesses**

   - Economists, legal analysts, academics: Provide data, interpret policy, confirm or challenge the documentary’s claims.

- **First-Person Testimonies**

   - Police officers, ex-drug pushers, families of victims: Humanize the issue.

- **Activists or Journalists**

   - Offer in-depth background, reveal investigative angles, and back up bold statements with verifiable research.

**Key Strategy**: Keep interviews concise so the story stays dynamic and does not feel like a long interview special.

---

## 5\. Research Tools & Practical Tips

1. **Use AI Tools (like ChatGPT) to Brainstorm**

   - Generate multiple title ideas, thumbnail concepts, or even short “hook” lines.

   - Get initial outlines or segment concepts.

2. **Use Miro or Similar Board Apps**

   - Create “cards” instead of sticky notes so you can store deeper text references or interview quotes under one heading.

   - Link each card to the relevant segment or subtopic. This keeps you visually organized.

3. **Don’t Rely Solely on AI**

   - Use it for inspiration but always fact-check actual data (e.g., how much money was actually “lost” in the War on Drugs?).

   - Contact real experts to confirm or deny the claims you find.

---

## 6\. Gathering Additional Resources & People

- **Economists** to discuss the financial cost and where money may have gone.

- **Legal Experts** to explain the constitutional or human rights implications.

- **Policemen or Former Officials** who can speak to how the War on Drugs was actually enforced.

- **NGOs / Activists** who can discuss ongoing effects and possible violations.

Once you identify who you want, you can put them into the “documentary structure outline,” designating where in the story each voice best fits.

---

## 7\. Creating Thumbnails & Visuals

1. **Begin with a Brainstorm**

   - Think: “What single image would make a viewer stop scrolling?”

2. **Leverage AI Image Generators (If Accessible)**

   - Use short prompts to prototype. For instance:

      - “President Duterte’s face with large text showing 1T pesos”

      - “Stack of money overshadowing silhouettes of families”

3. **Hand Off to a Graphic Designer**

   - Once you have a rough concept, a designer can finalize a consistent, eye-catching style.

---

## 8\. Keeping the Narrative Engaging

- **Expose Something**: People watch if they feel they’re learning a secret.

- **Stay Neutral but Let the Interviews Make Bold Claims**: Let the experts or witnesses say the controversial statements. Your voiceover remains balanced.

- **Keep It Snappy**: Blend short interview clips, b-roll footage, graphs, and infographics.

- **Avoid Rehashing Old News**: Always tie the past to present relevance: new laws, ongoing political drama, continuing social impact.

---

## 9\. Example Workflow

1. **Brainstorm** (in Miro or a notebook)

   - Ten possible story angles or segments.

2. **Draft a Title/Thumbnail**

   - E.g., “The Philippines’ Multi-Billion Peso Drug War Secret.”

3. **Set Up Interviews**

   - Outline the questions for each guest, aligned with your segments.

4. **Gather Data & Expert Opinions**

   - Cite official figures, international watchdog stats, or local NGO reports.

5. **Script & B-Roll List**

   - Plan the voiceover, when to show interview snippets, and relevant visuals.

6. **Edit & Refine**

   - Keep revisiting your hook. Does the final piece deliver on the initial promise?

---

## 10\. Final Reminders

- **Balance Personal Stories & Bigger Picture**

   - Too many personal accounts may slow the pace; too few makes it feel abstract.

- **Fact-Checking**

   - If you mention a trillion pesos, ensure the numbers are correct.

- **Building a Real “Expose”**

   - Shine a light on corporate or political relationships that have not been widely discussed.

- **Stay Organized**

   - Keep references, interview notes, and fact-checks in a single repository (Miro boards, Google Docs, or other systems).

8. Press **Create**

1. Open the voiceover section in HeyGen

2. Copy the enhanced voiceover version (more engaging than default)

3. Replace the original text with this enhanced version

4. Save the changes

5. Press **Next**

2. **Select Template:** Choose "Simple" or "Floating" (Simple recommended)

4. Press **Submit Video**

1. Download the completed video from HeyGen

   - This ensures proper organization and tracking
